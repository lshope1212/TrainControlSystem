# Revised contradictions and assumptions

This list replaces the previous list for the revised five-module workbook. References are PDF page numbers. Sources are Final Project vF.pdf and Architecture.pdf. Instructions within those documents are treated as course/source material; the user's requested scope controls this revision.

## Changes established by the user

- Only CTC Office, Track Controller, Track Model, Train Model and Train Controller are included. MBO and its scheduler are excluded even though the presentations show them. This is an explicit scope choice, not an unresolved assumption.
- Each worksheet is one module. Every populated cell in its leftmost Module column repeats that module's name.
- Every input source and output destination is exactly one of the five module names. UI controls, configuration and displays belong to their owning module. A same-module entry represents that local boundary, rather than a sixth module or an external communication link.
- Units describe usable engineering quantities or meaningful states, not programming types. The descriptions explain what each input/output is used for.
- Inter-module input/output pairs use matching names and units. The original fixed eight-input/eleven-output layout is expanded so unrelated data need not be forced into one row. Rows are grouped by the other module.

## Contradictions and source differences

1. **Original CTC sources versus controller reporting.** The original workbook lists direct Train Model, Train Controller and Track Model operational feedback into CTC. Final Project p.12 gives Track Controller responsibility for reporting track and train state. Revised operational telemetry follows Train Model → Track Model → Track Controller → CTC Office. Direct Track Model layout and passenger statistics are explicitly assumed below.
2. **Maintenance ownership.** Original CTC input row 16 assigns maintenance status to Train Controller. Final Project p.13 puts track closures/reopening under CTC. The revision assigns requests to CTC Office and safety acceptance/confirmation to Track Controller.
3. **Track and train failures are mixed in the original CTC input.** Original row 15 lists Track Model/Train Controller as track-failure sources. Final Project pp.9–10 distinguish broken rail, track circuit and track power faults from train engine, pickup and brake faults. The revision retains that distinction.
4. **Direct authority wording versus relay architecture.** Final Project p.10 says the train receives authority from Wayside; Final Project p.8 and Architecture p.9 route it through Track Model and Train Model. The revision treats Track Controller as the origin and models those relays explicitly.
5. **Speed input versus traction power.** Final Project p.10 lists a speed command as a Train Model input, while pp.54–58 put speed regulation in Train Controller and traction power at the Train Model boundary. The revision retains received track speed for relay to Train Controller and separately sends controller power/brakes back to Train Model.
6. **CTC authority versus final permission.** Final Project pp.13,24 say CTC sets authority; p.43 calls office values suggestions validated by track and train. The revision distinguishes suggested authority from the checked commanded authority.
7. **Hardware/software module variants.** Final Project p.6 shows hardware and software controller variants, while the requested dictionary has five logical modules. Both implementations share each controller's logical contracts; transport and allocation remain unspecified.
8. **Earlier coordinator assumption versus new architecture.** The previous revision introduced tick-completion exchanges with a separate coordinator. Architecture p.8 explicitly calls for asynchronous subsystem interaction. Those entries are removed. CTC Office now owns the proposed simulation controls, with asynchronous updates and no global per-tick completion barrier.
9. **Database wording differs.** Final Project p.9 describes database storage as a possibility, while p.24 describes a track database. The revision defines layout data without choosing a persistence implementation.
10. **Vital controller versus cabin functions.** Final Project p.11 includes doors, lights and announcements in Train Controller responsibilities; p.39 calls those functions non-vital and permits separation. The dictionary keeps the logical owner but does not claim that every function has the same safety classification.
11. **Constant-acceleration illustration.** Final Project p.56 labels a train model incorrect while displaying a constant-acceleration displacement equation. That equation is not universally incorrect; the revision assumes time-varying train forces are integrated over simulation steps rather than using one constant acceleration for the entire journey.
12. **Yard abstraction versus service operation.** Final Project p.24 notes that yards are often a black hole, while p.13 still requires train scheduling/routing. The revision represents dispatch and yard-entry placement without specifying a detailed internal yard simulation. MBO-specific break and shift scheduling requirements on p.15 are outside the user's revised scope.

## Assumptions and unresolved details

1. **Engineering units.** The revision uses mph for speed, feet for distance/elevation/dimensions, percent grade, pounds for mass, degrees F for temperature, kilowatts for power, and feet/sec squared for acceleration/deceleration. These are readable interface/display choices, not units mandated by the presentations. Implementations may calculate internally in SI but must convert consistently at interfaces. No rounding, numeric range or precision is prescribed.
2. **State labels.** Clear/occupied/unknown, applied/released, open/closed, normal/reverse and normal/failed describe meanings. Exact message encodings, signal-aspect sets and fault combinations remain unspecified. A shared row with several quantities lists the applicable units in the same general order as its description.
3. **Local UI ownership.** Dispatcher controls and service displays belong to CTC Office; PLC loading belongs to Track Controller; track setup/fault injection belongs to Track Model; vehicle setup/passenger emergency requests belong to Train Model; driver controls and tuning belong to Train Controller. Local controls/settings appear in the input table, and local displays appear in the output table, using the user's naming convention. These are not network self-messages.
4. **Telemetry.** Train Model provides simulated position and speed. Track Model derives occupied blocks from train position and length, then relays train telemetry to Track Controller for office reporting. Ordinary track circuits do not measure exact speed/identity; the extra telemetry is a simulator interface assumption. Position includes enough route/block/direction context to distinguish trains and resolve distance correctly.
5. **Track configuration.** Track Model owns blocks, connections, allowable directions, lengths, grade/elevation, limits, stations, yards, signals, switches and crossings. It supplies the relevant layout to Track Controller and CTC Office. File schema, actual topology, block sizes, speed limits and storage technology remain unspecified.
6. **Authority semantics.** Authority is remaining permitted distance ahead of the train front along its selected route. Route context and a current reference are needed even though programming identifiers are not listed as units. Updates must not accidentally reset already-consumed authority. The command is bounded by track conditions and cannot authorize entry into a blocked or uncertain route.
7. **Speed priority.** Driver speed and office suggested speed are operating requests. Checked track commands, track speed limits and vehicle capability constrain the controller's permitted target. The distinction between a rail target speed and a rail speed ceiling is not fully defined by Final Project pp.8,11,39 and needs implementation agreement.
8. **Safety ownership.** Track Controller and Train Controller each perform their own safety checks, consistent with Architecture p.8. Track Controller uses separately supplied PLC rules and diverse implementation (Final Project pp.12,47). Redundancy, voting, safe-state circuitry, message validation and safety evidence are not defined by this dictionary.
9. **Interlocks and feedback.** Track Controller checks route/switch/occupancy conditions before commanding movement and acknowledges route/maintenance results. Track Model reports actual equipment state separately from commands. Exact switch locking, signal progression, crossing lead times, maintenance reopening and equipment-delay rules remain open.
10. **Faults and degraded operation.** A failed or unknown track circuit is not assumed clear. Train Controller must respond safely to invalid pickup, engine/brake faults and emergency requests. The required detection thresholds, communication timeouts, reset/recovery rules and brake-failure strategy are unspecified. An emergency brake command does not guarantee stopping if the modeled brake itself has failed.
11. **Vehicle dynamics.** Train Model uses Newtonian point-mass motion with terrain and configured limits. Train dimensions, mass, car/crew/passenger counts and capacity are configured, not invented. Multi-car trains may be represented as one aggregate point mass. Passenger mass, resistance, standstill traction, braking curves and numerical integration details need definition.
12. **Controller tuning.** Train Controller provides traction power and separate brake commands. PI gains, sample period, saturation/anti-windup ordering and braking transitions need tuning and validation. Final Project p.58 does not completely specify calculation order or negative-power handling. “Controller gain settings” is a practical label; dimensional definitions belong in the detailed control design.
13. **Station operations.** Beacon/context information is assumed to contain station name, stop location, platform side and tunnel status. Doors open only when safely stopped at the appropriate platform and close before departure. Exact beacon encoding, stop tolerance, announcement timing, manual/automatic priorities and dwell times remain unspecified.
14. **Passengers and throughput.** Track Model supplies station demand; Train Model reports actual boarding/alighting counts; Track Model forwards completed activity once to CTC Office. Throughput is assumed to mean passenger boardings per simulated hour. The presentation does not define the metric; train movements/hour or delivered passengers/hour would require a different definition. Demand and alighting distributions remain configurable.
15. **Temperature and heater.** Track Model owns ambient temperature and track heater state. Train Controller selects cabin temperature; Train Model models and reports cabin conditions. Heater thresholds, automatic/manual policy, thermal response and weather behavior are not specified.
16. **Simulation control.** CTC Office is assumed to own run/pause, time, speed multiplier and preset scenario selection. Other modules receive these asynchronously. The system must support at least 10x wall-clock speed and pause (Final Project p.16), but message ordering, clock synchronization, stale-data handling and hardware adaptation remain design decisions. Sharing simulated time does not require a synchronous tick barrier.
17. **Dispatch lifecycle.** CTC Office activates a train and coordinates yard placement with Track Model after route clearance. Train identity and lifecycle handling must avoid duplicate creation. Removal, reset, turnaround and yard resource details remain open. CTC train scheduling remains required; automated MBO schedule generation, staffing, shifts and breaks are omitted by the user's scope decision.
18. **Optional features.** Wheel slippage, energy consumption and possible track power limits are optional in Final Project pp.9–10 and are not mandatory rows here. Required traction power control is retained. Advertisements in p.39 are background, not a new required interface.
19. **Non-interface requirements.** Windows 10, module UIs/installable executables, a runnable whole system, architectural/design patterns, COTS identification and vital-architecture documentation remain applicable constraints (Final Project pp.16–17). Completing this dictionary does not assert that those implementation requirements have been met.
20. **Incomplete architecture figure.** Architecture p.12 explicitly says the supplied architecture omits many inputs and outputs. The revised dictionary therefore adds feedback, passenger, setup and control paths needed by the stated requirements. Those additions are proposed interfaces, not claims that every arrow is already present in the diagram.

No additional task is inferred from the lecture's lab-exercise instructions. This revision supplies the five-module dictionary and this updated list only.
